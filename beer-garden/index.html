15th & Lombard

a beer garden, a vacant lot, and one very loaded block of Philadelphia

39.9445° N, 75.1667° W · a warm night, South Philly

· · · ✦ · · ·

You're sitting under string lights on the edge of the old Seventh Ward. You probably didn't order a history lesson. Here's one anyway — sip while you read.

01🍺The garden you're in

Odds are you're at the PHS Pop-Up Garden on South Street — technically just around the corner at 1438 South St, half a block south of Lombard. For most of the year it's a plain vacant lot. Then every summer the Pennsylvania Horticultural Society — yes, the people behind the Flower Show — roll in wood-chip floors, big leafy trees, hundreds of potted plants, and those string lights over your head, and turn the empty corner into a garden bar for a few warm months.

A patch of dirt that blooms into a bar and then disappears again. Very Philadelphia.

The food and cocktails have long been run with the crew from Royal Tavern, the plants are real, and the whole thing folds back up before the frost. It is, in the most literal sense, a temporary paradise — which turns out to rhyme nicely with the history of the block it's borrowed.

02📖You're drinking inside a famous map

Look north one block to Spruce, south to South Street, and you've just traced the top and bottom of the Seventh Ward — the neighborhood W.E.B. Du Bois walked door to door in 1896–97 for The Philadelphia Negro (1899), the first serious sociological study of an American city.

The ward ran from Spruce down to South, Seventh Street west to the Schuylkill — and 15th & Lombard sits squarely inside it. Du Bois and his wife Nina lived in the ward and canvassed thousands of Black households, hand-coloring a now-legendary block-by-block map of the very ground your table is standing on. It was the heart of Black Philadelphia, and this corner was right in the middle of it.

So when someone says "this is a historic block," they're underselling it. It's a data point in the founding document of American urban sociology.

03🛣️Why there are vacant lots to pop up in

Here's the twist that ties it together. In the 1950s and '60s, city planners drew an eight-lane Crosstown Expressway straight along South Street. The plan was to bulldoze the whole corridor for a highway.

It never got built — the neighborhood fought it for two decades and won, with a famous 1968 study by architects Denise Scott Brown & Robert Venturi arguing that South Street was too alive to pave. The expressway was finally deleted in 1974.

But the threat had already scared businesses off and cratered the rents — which is exactly why the corridor filled with cheap studios, oddball shops, artists… and empty lots that never got redeveloped.

One of those never-paved, never-rebuilt lots is the one your beer is currently sitting on. The garden exists because the highway didn't.

04🎶A song for the walk home

All that low-rent, last-minute energy had a soundtrack. In 1963 the Philly vocal group The Orlons cut "South Street" — "Where do all the hippies meet? South Street, South Street!" It hit #3 on the national charts and made this scruffy, half-condemned corridor famous a full decade before it was officially saved.

Cue it up on the walk back to 15th & Lombard. You've earned the outro.
